Water & Drainage

Water is the number-one enemy of buildings. Wood rots, metal rusts, paint peels, and mold grows when water gets where it shouldn't. Keeping a house dry is one of the most important jobs in building science.

Three kinds of water

  1. Bulk water — rain, snow, sprinklers, puddles. The big, obvious stuff.
  2. Capillary water — water that climbs through tiny pores in materials like concrete. (Think of how a paper towel soaks up a spill.)
  3. Water vapor — moisture floating in the air. You can't see it, but it's there.

How houses fight water

  • Roof and overhangs push rain away from the walls.
  • Gutters and downspouts catch rain and send it far from the foundation.
  • Grading — the slope of the ground — directs water away from the house.
  • Flashing — thin metal pieces — block water from sneaking in around windows, doors, and roofs.
  • Drainage plane — a hidden layer behind the siding that drains any water that gets past the outside.

The rules of water

Builders try to follow some simple rules: shed water at the top, drain it in the middle, and keep the bottom dry. A roof sheds. A wall drains. A foundation needs to stay dry. When a house follows these rules, it can last a very long time.

Did you know?

Most "rotten wood" you see in old houses isn't really a wood problem — it's a water problem. Wood that stays dry can last for hundreds of years!

Words to know

Flashing
Thin metal pieces that direct water away from joints in a house.
Grading
The slope of the ground around a house.
Foundation
The part of the house that sits on or in the ground and holds it up.
Downspout
The vertical pipe that carries water from gutters down to the ground.
